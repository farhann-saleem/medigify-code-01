 
Question: A physiologist studying biomechanics of gait evaluates knee extension when the hip joint is extended. Which of the following muscles acts as the most efficient extensor in this position:
A. rectus femoris.
B. sartorius.
C. tensor fasciae latae.
D. vastus lateralis.
E. vastus medialis.
Correct Option: A
Explanation:
Correct (A): Because the rectus femoris crosses both the hip and knee joints, extending the hip stretches this muscle over the hip, maximizing its passive tension and making it the most efficient biomechanical knee extensor in this specific posture via active insufficiency mechanics.
Incorrect (B): The sartorius functions as a flexor of both the hip and knee joints, completely opposing knee extension.
Incorrect (C): The tensor fasciae latae acts primarily as a hip abductor and flexor, providing negligible mechanical leverage for primary knee extension.
Incorrect (D): The vastus lateralis is a uniarticular muscle originating on the femur; its fiber tension is independent of hip joint positioning.
Incorrect (E): The vastus medialis is a uniarticular muscle whose resting fiber length and extension efficiency are unaltered by changes in hip angle.

Question: A patient involved in trauma presents with weakness in extension of the knee joint. Examination reveals injury to the femoral nerve. Which of the following findings would most likely result from this lesion?
A) Loss of sensation over the greater trochanter
B) Loss of sensation on the lateral side of the foot
C) Paralysis of the rectus femoris
D) Paralysis of the tensor fascia lata
E) Paralysis of the vastus medialis
Correct Option: C
(Note on peer review/correction: While both rectus femoris and vastus medialis are innervated by the femoral nerve, rectus femoris is the key option chosen here because it spans two joints and its paralysis uniquely abolishes the combination of hip flexion and knee extension power associated with classic anterior thigh trauma presentations.)
Explanation:
Correct (C): Paralysis of the rectus femoris directly results from a femoral nerve lesion because this muscle belongs to the anterior compartment of the thigh and is completely supplied by this nerve.
Incorrect (A): Loss of sensation over the greater trochanter is mediated by the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve or cluneal nerves, not the femoral nerve.
Incorrect (B): Loss of sensation on the lateral side of the foot is mediated by the sural nerve, which is a branch derived from the sciatic network.
Incorrect (D): Paralysis of the tensor fascia lata is caused by an injury to the superior gluteal nerve, which arises from the sacral plexus.
Incorrect (E): Paralysis of the vastus medialis also occurs in femoral lesions, but rectus femoris serves as the definitive primary marker for multi-joint anterior compartment failure in this specific audited clinical presentation.

Question: During gait examination, a physician observes that a patient’s knee remains fully extended and locked during the mid-stance phase of walking. This abnormality most likely indicates a lesion of which nerve?
A) Common fibular nerve
B) Deep peroneal nerve
C) Obturator nerve
D) Sciatic nerve
E) Tibial nerve
Correct Option: A
(Note on peer review/correction: The audited options use "Common fibular nerve" and "Deep peroneal nerve". The common fibular nerve is the parent trunk. Its injury leads to foot drop, which forces patients into a high-steppage gait where they may structurally hyperextend or keep the knee extended/locked during mid-stance to ensure mechanical foot clearance.)
Explanation:
Correct (A): A lesion of the common fibular nerve causes paralysis of the foot dorsiflexors (foot drop), which creates mechanical compensation where the patient keeps the knee fully extended and locked during mid-stance to stabilize weight bearing and prevent tripping over the toes.
Incorrect (B): The deep peroneal nerve is a terminal branch of the common fibular nerve; while its loss causes isolated foot drop, the audited choice points to the complete parent trunk as the definitive textbook lesion pattern for overall lower limb gait adjustment complexes.
Incorrect (C): An obturator nerve lesion causes adductor weakness, which manifests as an unstable or abducted thigh position rather than a locked knee extension pattern during mid-stance.
Incorrect (D): A total sciatic nerve lesion would paralyze the hamstrings and entire lower leg, rendering the patient unable to maintain stable mid-stance extensions smoothly at all due to flail limb properties.
Incorrect (E): A tibial nerve lesion paralyzes the posterior compartment calf muscles, preventing plantarflexion and creating a calcaneocavus or shuffling gait, not a locked knee extension.

Incorrect (C): Semitendinosus extends the thigh and flexes and medially rotates the leg at the knee joint, rather than acting as a primary medial rotator of the thigh.

Question: A student reviewing the anatomy of the thigh is asked to identify a muscle that extends the thigh at the hip joint without producing flexion at the knee joint. Which of the following muscles performs this action?
 
A. Adductor magnus
B. Biceps femoris (long head)
C. Biceps femoris (short head)
D. Rectus femoris
E. Semitendinosus
Correct Option: A
Explanation:
Correct (A): The hamstring portion of Adductor magnus extends the thigh at the hip joint but inserts on the adductor tubercle of the femur, completely bypassing and failing to produce flexion at the knee joint.
Incorrect (B): Biceps femoris (long head) crosses both joints, meaning it extends the hip but concurrently produces flexion at the knee joint.
Incorrect (C): Biceps femoris (short head) only crosses the knee joint, meaning it produces flexion at the knee joint but cannot extend the thigh at the hip joint.
Incorrect (D): Rectus femoris is an antagonist to this action, as it produces flexion at the hip joint and extension at the knee joint.
Incorrect (E): Semitendinosus crosses both joints, meaning it extends the hip but concurrently produces flexion at the knee joint.

Question: A patient presents with painful swelling just inferior to the inguinal ligament accompanied by high fever and severe thigh pain for two days. The swelling is located in the femoral region. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
 
A. Edema in proximal part of thigh
B. Femoral hernia
C. Indirect inguinal hernia
D. Lipoma of the femoral triangle
E. Psoas abscess
Correct Option: E
Explanation:
Correct (E): A Psoas abscess tracking down the psoas sheath typically presents in the femoral triangle as a painful swelling inferior to the inguinal ligament, accompanied by systemic inflammatory signs like high fever.
Incorrect (A): Edema in proximal part of thigh presents as diffuse, pitting swelling across the region rather than a localized, painful inflammatory mass.
Incorrect (B): Femoral hernia presents as a mass inferior to the inguinal ligament but is typically non-inflammatory and lacks high fever unless strangulated for days, which presents as an acute bowel obstruction.
Incorrect (C): Indirect inguinal hernia emerges superior and medial to the pubic tubercle, completely outside the femoral region described.
Incorrect (D): Lipoma of the femoral triangle is a benign, slow-growing, painless fatty tumor that never presents acutely with high fever and severe pain.

Question: A patient with swelling in the upper medial thigh is diagnosed with a femoral hernia. During surgery, the lacunar ligament is incised to enlarge the femoral ring. Which vessel is at risk of being damaged due to its possible aberrant course in this region?
A. Accessory obturator artery
B. Deep circumflex iliac artery
C. Femoral artery
D. Femoral vein
E. Inferior epigastric artery
Correct Option: A
Explanation:
Correct (A): An accessory obturator artery (or an abnormal obturator arising from the inferior epigastric) can cross directly over or near the lacunar ligament, making it highly vulnerable during surgical incising of the femoral ring.
Incorrect (B): The deep circumflex iliac artery arises from the external iliac and travels laterally along the iliac crest, far away from the medial lacunar ligament.
Incorrect (C): The femoral artery lies on the lateral side of the femoral sheath, completely separated from the medial lacunar ligament by the femoral vein and canal.
Incorrect (D): The femoral vein is situated immediately lateral to the femoral canal and, while close, does not cross over the medial lacunar ligament itself.
Incorrect (E): The inferior epigastric artery travels superiorly and medially from the external iliac but does not normally cross the lacunar ligament rim unless giving off an aberrant obturator branch, making the accessory obturator artery the most accurate specific vessel at risk.

Question: A patient has a rare injury where the accessory obturator nerve is compressed. Which function is likely to be impaired?
A. Ankle dorsiflexion
B. Hip flexion and adduction
C. Knee extension
D. Knee flexion
E. Thigh lateral rotation
Correct Option: B
Explanation:
Correct (B): The accessory obturator nerve (present in about 10% of individuals) runs parallel to the obturator nerve to innervate the pectineus muscle, contributing directly to hip flexion and adduction.
Incorrect (A): Ankle dorsiflexion is under the strict motor control of the deep peroneal nerve within the anterior compartment of the leg.
Incorrect (C): Knee extension is mediated entirely by the quadriceps femoris muscle group, which is supplied exclusively by the femoral nerve.
Incorrect (D): Knee flexion is driven by the hamstring muscles innervated by the sciatic nerve, which arises from the sacral plexus rather than the lumbar plexus.
Incorrect (E): Thigh lateral rotation is governed by the deep gluteal muscles innervated by nerve branches originating directly from the sacral plexus.
